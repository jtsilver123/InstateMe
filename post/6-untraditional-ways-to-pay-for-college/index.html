6 Untraditional Ways to Pay for College

By Jake Silver·Sep 15, 2022·5 min read·Updated Sep 22, 2022

Read on to learn the sweet ways to afford college!

Context

Sure, we all know the usual cookie-cutter ways to help lessen the cost of ever-increasing college tuition: you can cross your fingers for scholarships and pray for favorable loans and grants through FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid). But securing a meaningful scholarship is a highly competitive affair among the cream of the crop with the highest SAT/ACT scores. And the best grants and free money? That’s reserved primarily for those with the most extraordinary circumstances.

What about the rest of us? Let’s think outside the box. You don’t need to be a valedictorian to afford college. These untraditional ways to pay for college help level the playing field and might just help you avoid a mountain of debt.

1) Residency

It’s possible to earn in-state tuition in any state; find out if it’s viable for you.

This is first at bat because (if attending an out-of-state college) you can actually combine it with a few of the other non-traditional approaches below, leading to an even bigger return on your time. For example, you can work toward in-state residency while completing a gap year — you save money during the gap, and you begin or return to college with residency and in-state tuition. Win-win!

Most people do not realize that if you qualify as a resident of the state in the eyes of a university, you are entitled to in-state resident tuition — averaging over $90,000 in total cost difference over a four-year college education. Keep in mind that the rules vary drastically by state and by school. One of the easiest resources for exploring in-state tuition is InstateMe, where you’ll find general info for free as well as time-and-energy-saving services for even more assistance.

2) American Opportunity Tax Credit

If you become financially independent, you may qualify for free money.

The American Opportunity Tax Credit (AOTC) is a credit for qualified education expenses paid for an eligible student for the first four years of higher education. You can get a maximum annual credit of $2,500 per eligible student. If the credit brings the amount of tax you owe to zero, you can have 40 percent of any remaining amount of the credit (up to $1,000) refunded to you.

The amount of the credit is 100 percent of the first $2,000 of qualified education expenses you paid for each eligible student and 25 percent of the next $2,000.

3) The CLEP

A lesser-known gem — CLEP stands for College-Level Examination Program.

CLEP is a sister to AP (Advanced Placement) courses taken for college credit, but CLEP is available to anyone, with no prerequisites needed. CLEP offers dozens of exams in introductory college-level subjects (Financial Accounting, Biology, American Literature, Introductory Sociology, Spanish, Calculus, and more) that allow a person, if they pass, to obtain anywhere from 3 to 12 college credits per exam.

You can prepare for the exams free of charge, testing centers are located throughout the country and internationally, and each computer-administered exam costs about $80. If you don’t pass, there’s a 6-month waiting period before a retake. A dedicated and studious teenager could graduate high school with their college prerequisites completed — if not an earned associate’s degree — at a much lower cost than earning the same credits over two years at college.

Every college is different, so if you’re considering CLEP, first cross-reference with your college of choice to make sure the credits will transfer and are required for your major.

4) Crowdfunding

Yes, there really are people out there who will provide you money… for free!

So go ahead and tell your story. Plead your case. Simply ask for help from other people. Crowdfunding platforms have raised billions of dollars. Take the time to ask family, friends, alumni, and even strangers. Among the leaders is GoFundMe, but there are a variety of others worth considering.

One word of caution: if you are planning to petition for residency and in-state tuition at a university, make sure the school allows you to accept funds and/or gifts via crowdfunding. Not sure? Check with the university!

5) International studies

Perhaps one of the most untraditional ways to pay for college… but why!? It’s so clever.

Many students partake in study-abroad programs and semesters at sea — so why not extend that a bit, get unbeatable outside-the-box experience, and earn your degree for a lot less, if not free? In Germany, for example, higher education is free for students — including international students! Instead of paying tuition, your costs would cover flights, room, board, and living expenses. You may even receive free healthcare depending on the country and school.

The caveat: do your due diligence. Ensure the school abroad offers courses/credits that transition back to the United States smoothly, double-check that classes are offered in English, scope out the application process and requirements, and make sure it is an accredited institution. Many schools abroad offer assistance to Americans looking to study internationally, including U.S.-based representatives and transition support services.

6) Non-traditional year

Forget the straight-to-college-three-months-after-high-school approach — consider a community college stint or a productive gap year.

With community college, you pay far less while maintaining momentum and earning credits that transfer to a university. Many universities allow one-year deferrals if you decide to take the year off or attend community college. Ahead of time, make certain which courses/credits will transfer smoothly. Community college is a great way to rack up prerequisites at a much lower cost.

When it comes to a gap year, there are a variety of routes:

  • Take a year off and lounge on your parents’ couch (not the normally accepted option).
  • If planning to attend an in-state university after your gap, use the year to work, earn income and savings, travel, get an internship in your field of study, or take the extra time to figure out exactly what you want to pursue.
  • If planning to attend an out-of-state university after your gap, strongly consider moving to your chosen destination for the gap year. Get to know and enjoy the area, get a job and make money you otherwise would not have, go on informational interviews — and make it a productive year (it can also start your residency clock).
