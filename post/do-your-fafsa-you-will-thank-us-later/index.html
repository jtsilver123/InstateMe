Do Your FAFSA… You Will Thank Us Later

By Jake Silver·Sep 15, 2022·3 min read·Updated Sep 22, 2022

Read on to learn what the FAFSA is and its importance in your financial journey.

What is the FAFSA?

FAFSA stands for Free Application for Federal Student Aid — a form that prospective and current college students in the U.S. can complete to see what financial aid they qualify for to help pay for the cost of college. The financial aid offered can be in the form of favorable loans, grants, or scholarships from the U.S. government, the state, or the university, and varies depending on the student’s financial needs.

Why should I do it?

Almost all students qualify for at least some sort of financial aid via completion of the FAFSA. Many people don’t realize that much university-specific aid is apportioned only to those who submit a FAFSA — i.e., you run the risk of foregoing totally free money by not doing it.

Isn’t it really hard to do?

Some people say the FAFSA is too hard, but it really is not. You can complete it for free online at fafsa.gov, or you can have somebody help you complete it over the phone with paid assistance services.

Is it true that if parents make a lot of money you can’t get financial aid?

Some people say the FAFSA is a waste of time because their parents make too much money — but that’s not quite the case:

  • Even wealthy families are typically offered at least favorable government loans.
  • Many universities have their own institutional grants and scholarships they only award to those who complete the FAFSA — and these can change from year to year.
  • There is a 100% chance of not getting any free money by not doing the FAFSA, but a better-than-zero chance of getting some by doing it.

When should I do it?

Now! ASAP! Complete the FAFSA as soon as possible because financial aid packages are awarded on a first-come, first-served basis. Submit early so you’re first in line for awards — deadlines fluctuate slightly from year to year, so check the current cycle’s dates.

What happens after I fill out the FAFSA?

After completing the free application, you receive an award summary (the SAR — Student Aid Report) that details what financial aid you are offered. Then you choose the financial aid offerings that work best for you.

What will I be offered?

Financial aid is offered on a case-by-case basis; the most common types include:

Favorable loans

  • Subsidized Stafford Loan — 0% interest while the student is in school; interest begins accruing after graduation.
  • Unsubsidized Stafford Loan — interest begins accruing immediately but no payment is due until after graduation.
  • Federal Perkins Loan — similar to Stafford loans but lent by the school.
  • Federal Work-Study Program — part-time work where the school and federal government share the wages.
  • PLUS (Parent Loan for Undergraduate Students) — borrowed by the parent(s) of the student.

Grants & scholarships

  • Residency-based grants or scholarships.
  • Merit-based awards for high academic achievement.

Very important: FAFSA and residency / in-state tuition

If a student is looking to earn residency and in-state tuition, it is crucial to fully understand the specific school’s regulations when it comes to financial aid via the FAFSA and the awards offered.

Many times a PLUS loan will disqualify a student from earning in-state tuition where the school’s financial requirements mandate the student be wholly or partially financially self-sufficient. Any financial aid — including grants and scholarships — that requires the student to be a tax dependent of someone, or requires them to be an out-of-state resident, will in general disqualify them from establishing residency. By accepting such awards, the student gets no closer to in-state tuition. There are some exceptions whereby certain awards can be accepted during the domicile period but are automatically forgone upon earning in-state status (due to the much lower tuition owed then).

Financial aid is re-assessed after earning in-state status, and the Expected Family Contribution is considered in light of the vastly reduced tuition owed.
